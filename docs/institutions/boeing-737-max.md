## Q1 · Incentives

The person frame went hunting for bad engineers and greedy executives, and found material, as it always does. The load-bearing fact was structural. The FAA, underfunded and outmatched, had delegated much of the certification work back to Boeing itself, which was under schedule pressure to beat Airbus to market. The incentive map, a company grading its own safety homework against a deadline, predicts the outcome without needing a villain.

## Q2 · Corrections

The correction machinery did not fire from inside. The defect surfaced through two crashes, Lion Air in 2018 and Ethiopian Airlines in 2019, that killed 346 people. And the warning had been on the record years earlier: a 2012 DOT Inspector General report found that some employees in the FAA office overseeing Boeing felt they could not raise compliance concerns without fear of retaliation. Errors that can only surface through catastrophe are the opposite of a working corrections loop.

## Q3 · Verification

Under the Organization Designation Authorization program, the FAA delegated a large share of certification back to Boeing. So the entity meant to independently verify the aircraft was, in practice, substantially the manufacturer. That is verification in name, not in the structural sense the third question asks for.

## Q4 · Exit

Airlines and passengers do have an alternative in Airbus, but the commercial-jet market is a duopoly, and switching is expensive: fleets are standardized, pilots are type-rated to specific aircraft, and the 2019 grounding left carriers stuck with MAX jets they could neither fly nor easily replace. Real but costly exit.
